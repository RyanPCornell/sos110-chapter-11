Greenhouse Gases & the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Sunlight reaches Earth mostly as visible & near-infrared light; Earth radiates that energy back to space as thermal infrared. Greenhouse gases are nearly transparent to incoming sunlight but absorb specific bands of outgoing infrared — trapping heat. Key idea The 15 µm CO₂ band

Earth's surface (~15 °C) radiates most strongly around 10 µm, deep in the thermal infrared. CO₂ has a powerful absorption band centered at 15 µm — right where Earth emits a large share of its heat. That overlap is the physical heart of CO₂-driven warming.

Watch for The atmospheric window

Between roughly 8 and 13 µm the main gases absorb weakly, so heat escapes to space through this “window.” Methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O), and human-made halocarbons absorb inside this window — which is part of why they are such potent greenhouse gases per molecule.

Try this

① Turn on only CO₂ and raise the concentration slider — notice the 15 µm band deepens and widens, absorbing more of Earth's emission curve.

② Add H₂O: water vapor absorbs across broad regions and is Earth's most abundant greenhouse gas — but humans don't control it directly.

③ Add CH₄ and N₂O: their bands land partly in the 8–13 µm window, so small amounts matter.

④ Compare the absorption bands with the yellow sunlight curve — greenhouse gases barely touch incoming visible light. That asymmetry is the greenhouse effect.
